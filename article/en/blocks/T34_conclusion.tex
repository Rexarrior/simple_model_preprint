This article examined the transition from the local duration of an
AI-assisted task to the makespan of a fixed set of interdependent
software-development tasks. For a single human--agent cell, the answer to the
question posed in the Introduction is as follows: a configuration reduces the
makespan relative to sequential no-AI work not merely because multiple agents
are present, but only when a feasible schedule completes the same workload to
an accepted result faster than the baseline. Achievable speedup is jointly
determined by normalized task durations, task dependencies and phase profiles,
the number of agent streams and the rules governing their use, integration
overhead, and the sequential resource of human attention. This supports the
central proposition: speedup is a property of a fully specified process, not
an isolated characteristic of the AI tool or the nominal number of agents.

The first result---an operational comparison framework---delineates the scope
of this conclusion. The coefficient $k_i^{(r)}$ describes the duration until
an accepted result is obtained for a particular ``task---tool---mode'' triple
and has no causal interpretation without a separate identification design. The
configuration $\sigma=(P,\rho)$ fixes the number of streams and the mode
assignment, but a valid comparison of scenarios also requires the
decomposition, DAG, phase profiles, acceptance criterion, and overhead
functions either to be held fixed or to be varied explicitly. This distinction
precludes transferring a measured $k$ from interactive mode to a delegated
configuration with a different $P$, and distinguishes an autonomous agent
phase, during which the human is free, from fully autonomous mode.

The second result---the M0--M4 hierarchy---shows how the strength of the answer
changes as idealizations are relaxed. At M0, speedup is achieved if and only if
$P>\bar{k}_w$. At M1--M2, this condition remains necessary, but indivisibility
and dependencies introduce an additional gap; for an integer number of
identical streams and $C(P)=1$, a sufficient guarantee for a greedy schedule is
$\bar{k}_w<P-(P-1)\mu$, where $\mu=M_N/T_h$ for M1 and $\mu=L/T_h$ for M2. At
M3, the effective weights include cross-stream integration overhead. At M4, a
fully specified scenario accelerates the work if and only if its optimal
makespan satisfies $T^*<T_h$. If the structural lower bound $B_4\ge T_h$,
speedup is impossible; if $B_4<T_h$, this condition is not yet sufficient. A
feasible schedule with $T(\pi)<T_h$ is a sufficient certificate of speedup. If
the optimization problem is solved to certified optimality, the exact solver
establishes $T^*$ and therefore whether speedup is attainable.

The third result further characterizes the structure of M4. The lower bound

\[
  B_4=\max\left\{\frac{W_4(P)}{P},\;L_4(P),\;\gamma(P)H\right\}
\]

combines the aggregate load on the agent streams, the critical path in the
original task-DAG, and the single human resource. Repeated visits by tasks to
the developer and the retention of agent slots by waiting tasks, however,
create mixed resource chains that are absent from all three branches. The
resource-augmented phase graph represents the exact optimum as the minimum,
over feasible assignments and resource orders, of the maximum-weight path
length. This
strictly distinguishes the structural lower bound $B_4$, the certified optimum
$T^*$, and the makespan $T(\pi)$ of a selected policy. Sequential human work
also imposes an independent ceiling,
$S_E^{\mathrm{ach}}\le1/[\gamma(P)\bar h_w]\le1/\bar h_w$: once this ceiling is
reached, adding agents cannot substitute for shortening human-attention phases,
automating review, or formalizing the acceptance procedure.

The fourth result---the EXP-00--EXP-08 series---checks these distinctions on
reproducible scenarios. Illustrative Variants 2--4 yielded certified optima,
but the equality $T^*=B_4$ proved to be specific to those scenarios: in EXP-01,
a strict gap was found in 30 of 90 exact scenarios. In EXP-03, different phase
profiles with identical $A,H,W_4,L_4$, and $B_4$ yielded different values of
$T^*$ in 14 of 24 pairs. With durations held constant, additional available
capacity did not worsen the optimum, whereas increasing $C(P)$ or $\gamma(P)$
produced an interior minimum over configured $P$ on the EXP-02 curves examined.
Positive decomposition overhead produced a finite optimal granularity in 12 of
the 13 EXP-04 curves examined. In EXP-05, among the 100 interior comparisons
with positive overhead, the interaction contrast between parallelism and decomposition was positive in 96
and zero in four,
but the absolute effect of decomposition remained negative at 26 points.
Complementarity between parallelism and granularity therefore does not imply
that decomposition is unconditionally beneficial.

The robustness checks delimit the strength of these conclusions. Scale
invariance was reproduced at every EXP-06A point, and the advantage of
illustrative Variant 4 over Variant 3 was preserved within the specified random
and adverse error families of EXP-06B--C. The synthetic EXP-07 pilot supported
aggregate directions for a fixed policy, but not universal signs for every DAG
or claims about $T^*$. The corrected paired design of EXP-08 showed that total
human work determines the resource ceiling, whereas its placement affects path
lengths only when agent and human-attention phases have different effective
scaling multipliers; phase order can nevertheless change the queue and the
fixed-policy makespan even when the aggregates remain unchanged. All reported frequencies
refer to frozen synthetic matrices and are not estimates of the prevalence of
these effects in real projects.

Practical application of the model therefore does not begin by selecting the
maximum number of agents. For the scenarios being compared, tasks and operating
modes must be estimated on a common scale, the acceptance criterion must be
fixed, the DAG and phase profiles must be constructed, and $B_4$ must be
computed as a diagnostic of necessary constraints. An exact or heuristic
resource-feasible schedule must then be constructed, and sensitivity to $k$,
$h$, $C$, and $\gamma$ must be evaluated. The active branch of $B_4$ identifies
the constraint governing the lower bound, but extending this conclusion to the
actual optimum requires either a certificate that $B_4$ is attainable or an
exact analysis of the resource-augmented schedule. In this sense, the model is intended primarily
to compare ways of organizing work and to identify levers for changing the
makespan, not to promise an absolute prediction without local calibration.

The answer obtained is limited to the assumed architecture: a fixed, known
DAG; one developer; homogeneous agent streams; nonpreemptive phases; and local
slot retention. The model does not evaluate code quality and cost as separate
objectives and does not replace a causal study of AI effectiveness. The next
steps are to measure agent and human-attention intervals, queues, rework,
$C(P)$, and $\gamma(P)$ empirically using real traces; to proceed to interval
and stochastic calibration; and then to extend the formulation to dynamic DAG
revelation, adaptive decomposition, and other orchestration architectures.
Until such validation is performed, the central conclusion remains structural
but already operational: useful parallelism arises not from the number of
agents launched, but from the coordinated design of tasks, dependencies,
phases, resources, and acceptance rules for the human--agent process as a
whole.
